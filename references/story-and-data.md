# Story and data

## Start with the business shift

A prototype that only shows one person getting through a task faster is a productivity demo.  Aim higher: show
how the business could work differently once people and agents work together, and what that would be worth.  The
person's day is how the room sees it; the business shift is why they care.

**Tredence's point of view is the last mile**: insight matters when it changes a decision someone makes every day.
Most customers already have the data, the models and the dashboards; the forecast exists, and the order still gets
placed by gut feel.  So look for the decision that doesn't change today, and show the agent putting the answer in
front of the person who makes it, at the moment they make it, with the person still deciding.  That moment is
usually the signature moment; the business shift is what happens when every such decision changes.

**Answer these in `brief.md` before the storyboard:**

- **The tension.**  What limits this business today?  Not a task pain, a business constraint: who gets served and
  who doesn't, how fast it can respond, what it costs to serve, what slips through, what it can't offer.  Name it
  in their words.
- **Why it's stuck.**  Usually the work behind it is manual, scarce or slow, so the business rations it: the top
  accounts get attention, a sample gets checked, the customer waits.
- **The turn.**  What changes when agents do that work and people keep the decisions.  The rationing stops.
- **From → to.**  How the business works today and how it could work, one line each, in operating terms (who is
  served, how often, how fast, by whom), not features.
- **The measure that moves**, roughly how far, and its basis (volume, minutes, rate, your assumption).  Revenue,
  growth, retention, cost to serve, cycle time, risk caught, customer experience.
- **Who owns it.**  The leader accountable for that measure.  They are the real audience, even when the story
  follows someone else.

If "from → to" describes one person's task getting faster, it isn't a shift yet.  Ask what the business would do
with that time, reach or speed, and keep going until the answer changes how the business runs.

### Shifts (ideas, not templates)

Ways a business can change shape.  Use them to find this customer's shift; they say nothing about screens.

- **Serve everyone like your best customers**: attention only the top accounts get today reaches the whole book.
- **Ahead of the customer**: act before they ask, before the risk lands, before they think of leaving.
- **Weeks to days**: a cycle (onboarding, a quote, a claim, a close) shrinks, and that changes what the business
  can promise.
- **Every case, not a sample**: checks that were sampled now run on everything.
- **Decide at the front line**: the evidence comes to the person facing the customer; the decision doesn't wait
  for a hand-off.
- **One flow across teams**: the hand-offs between silos disappear, and nothing is lost between them.
- **A new offer**: something too costly to deliver by hand becomes a service the business can sell.
- **The same team, more reach**: capacity for growth or better work, never framed as cuts.

### Two heights

Show the shift **on the ground** (one person, one case, the moment the work changes, a human decision) and **from
above** (what that means across the business: the whole book, the region, the network, the measure moving, the
leader's view).  Many stories open on the business tension, go down to the moment, and come back up.  Without the
view from above, the room sees a faster day.  Without the ground, they see a claim.

### When the notes are a list of capabilities

Customers and colleagues often hand over a list of features or areas to cover.  Don't build one scene per item,
and don't tick them off at the end.  Ask what business result the list is for, tell that story, and let each
capability show up where it matters.  Keep a mapping of items to scenes in `brief.md` for the presenter, not on
screen.  If an item doesn't fit the story, say so in your reply rather than bolting on a scene.

## The storyboard

A prototype is a short story told through product screens.  Start in the customer's world, show the work as it
is today in one line, let the agent take the groundwork off people's plates while they stay in control, and show
what that lets the business do that it couldn't before.

**Then find the story on the ground.**  Answer these in `brief.md`:

- **Whose work shows the shift**, and where does that work start today (an inbox, a phone in the field, a
  meeting, a queue in their own system)?
- **What's slow, risky or tedious about it now?**  Name it in their words.
- **What does the agent take off their plate, and what do they still decide?**
- **What is the one moment that makes this customer lean in?**
- **What should the room say, do or ask for when the last scene goes down?**

**Then one sentence**: the shift, and the moment that shows it: who, what the agent does, what the person
decides, and what changes for the business.  Say it aloud.  If you can't say it in one sentence, the prototype
will wander.

**Then the beats**: what the room must see happen, in order.  **Then the scene table** (in `brief.md`), choosing
for each beat the screen that shows it best (`look-and-feel.md` §What screens mean).  The format:

| # | Screen | Presenter says (one line, out loud) | On screen | Click |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | |
| … | | | | |

There is no default sequence of screens.  Open where the tension is clearest or where this person's work really
starts, give the signature moment the view that shows it best, show the business from above, and end on the
shift.  A frame cover and close are common, not required.

Rules:
- **As many scenes as the story needs to be shown, not told.**  A demo is not a slide deck: if seeing the
  follow-through, the proof in the target system, or the hand-off to a second person makes the story believable,
  give it its own scene.  Typical: 5–8 for a single scenario, 10–14 for an end-to-end process.  Each scene must earn
  its place by showing something new happen; cut scenes that only repeat a point or explain instead of show.
- **About 30–60 seconds per scene**, one idea per scene.  For more than about 10 scenes, add a chapter break
  (`065.jpg`) between acts so the room keeps its place, and make the cover say what the acts are.
- **Every agent scene has a visible human decision**: confirm, edit, approve, choose.  This is what makes it
  credible and responsible.
- **Open where the work starts.**  Copilot Home, a phone in the field, an email arriving, a Teams meeting, the
  customer's own app or website: whatever is true for this person.  Employees work with the agent in the Copilot
  app, with business apps coming to them (sources, the Workspace pane, the browser pane); other apps appear where
  the work starts, as proof, or to show people reached.  Most stories need two or three supporting views, not a
  tour of every app.
- **Show the work, then the result.**  Work steps (what it read and checked) → result (with sources) → what changed.
- **One signature moment**: the specific thing that makes this customer lean in (a conflict it spotted, a gap it
  caught, a hand-off it saved).  Give it a view designed for it, build the beat timing around it, and give it the
  `.ai-touched` treatment.
- **Sound it out.**  Read every presenter line and every on-screen sentence aloud.  Calm, specific, honest.  If a
  line would make the presenter wince ("our AI eliminates manual work"), rewrite it.

### Story shapes (ideas, not templates)

Ways a story can move.  Mix them, or invent the one this customer needs; they say nothing about which screens to use.

- **Infer, confirm, act**: the agent drafts from signals; the person confirms; the record changes.
- **Prepare, then brief**: the agent gathers context across systems before a meeting or a visit.
- **Watch, then nudge**: an agent notices a risk (a date slips, an owner is missing) and asks before it acts.
- **Intake, then route**: a request arrives; the agent classifies it, fills the record, routes it to a person.
- **While you were away**: the agent worked overnight; the person reviews only the exceptions.
- **Two sides of one moment**: the customer's view and the employee's view of the same event.
- **Hand-off chain**: one case passes between three people, and nothing is lost at each hand-off.
- **Today and tomorrow**: the same task shown as it is now and as it could be, side by side.
- **Run the playbook**: one request runs a process; the plan shows who acts on each step; it stops once for
  input and once for approval; then proof.  Write the playbook in `brief.md` first (each step, who acts and why,
  what proof it leaves and where), taken from the customer's real process, not from what looks impressive.  One
  input and one approval is enough to make the point; more becomes a form.

## Starting from an existing HTML file or app

- Large single-file apps keep their content as data inside the script.  Pull that data out with a short Python
  script instead of reading the whole file.
- Use the card in `SKILL.md` (step 1), with the file's answers filled in, plus up to two lines it raises: which
  story to tell from the file and how it should end.
- If the file already uses its own fictional names, keep them unless the card says otherwise (add a line "Names:
  keep the file's").
- Leave out the file's own banners and disclaimers; the concept label covers them.
- Rebuild the scenes with the kit's classes and the product outlines (`assets/shells.html`) rather than copying
  the old markup, so the result looks and packs like every other prototype.

## The data sheet

Write the cast and the numbers once, in `brief.md`, and reuse them exactly.  Inconsistent data (a deal that's
$1.2M in Outlook and $1.4M in Dynamics) is the fastest way to lose the room.

```
Shift:    from [how the business works today] → to [how it could work]
Measure:  [measure] · today [value] → with agents [value] · illustrative · basis: [volume, minutes, rate, assumption]
Owner:    [role] (the leader accountable for the measure)
Company:  [fictitious customer] · [industry] · [size]
People:   [name], [role] (whose work changes)
          [name], [role]   (one line per person on screen)
Records:  [record type] "[name]" · [the fields the screens show, with their exact values]
Dates:    "today" = [weekday date]; [the other dates the story mentions]
Counts:   [what the agent read, found, drafted and changed, as numbers]
Charts:   [report] · [visual] · [series] · [categories] · [values] · [format]
```

### Making data feel real

- **Specific over round.**  $1,240,000, not $1M.  6 emails, not "all your emails".  "Since 28 Oct".  (Records and
  counts; impact estimates are rounded, below.)
- **Realistic texture.**  A mix of read and unread mail, a few older items, a field or two that's blank, a task
  with no owner yet.  Perfect data looks fake.
- **Dates line up** with "today" in the data sheet; times look like working hours; weekdays match dates.
- **Industry-true artefacts.**  Claims, work orders, policies, SKUs, shifts: use the customer's vocabulary.
- **Business impact is shown, honestly.**  The measure that moves appears (from above, in a Power BI visual or
  on the close), rounded ("about 3× the clients covered", "days, not weeks"), labelled "Illustrative" on screen,
  with its basis in `brief.md` and the presenter notes.  Plausible enough that a sceptical finance lead nods.
  Never present it as measured.  Time freed is capacity for more or better work, not a cash saving or a cut in
  staff.  Task-level numbers (minutes saved on one step) support the shift; they aren't the headline.
- **Charts are Power BI.**  Trends, gaps and breakdowns come from a named report and show as Power BI visuals.
  The number in the sentence, the visual, its tooltip and the report page are the same number from the data
  sheet.  One message per visual; put the series, categories and format in the data sheet.

## Words

The Tredence customer voice applies to every word on screen and in the notes (`tredence-frame.md` §Voice): start
in the customer's world, say what changes in someone's day, "we" and "you" together, confident but never
promotional, no exclamation marks.

- Short, plain, specific.  Product UI text is sentence case and functional ("Confirm plan", not "Let's go!").
- The agent speaks like a careful colleague: what it did, what it found, what it needs from you.  It admits gaps.
- Banned: unlock, unleash, seamless, leverage, utilise, supercharge, revolutionise, game-changing, empower,
  next-gen, cutting-edge, harness, delve, synergy, journey, flywheel, ideate, operationalise, best-in-class,
  world-class, magic.  The check flags them.
- Write "Tredence", never "TREDENCE" (that's the logo), "Tredence Inc." or "Tredence Analytics".  Tredence's own
  products and accelerators appear only if the user asks, described by what they do, never as a working product.
- No Tredence client names, client results, quotes or awards, even as background colour.  Proof points belong in a
  `pptx-tredence` deck, sourced and dated, not in a concept demo.
- Real product names exactly: Microsoft Copilot, Microsoft Teams, Outlook, Dynamics 365 Sales, Copilot Studio,
  Microsoft Fabric, Power BI, SharePoint.  Never invent product names or features as if they ship.
- Names change; check the current one before you build.  The Microsoft 365 Copilot app became **Microsoft
  Copilot** in August 2026 (copilot.cloud.microsoft): never write "Microsoft 365 Copilot" or "M365 Copilot".
  The Microsoft 365 subscription and Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and Teams keep their names.
- Internal Microsoft systems (MSX and similar) only for an internal audience, and only if the user asks.  For a
  customer, show the customer-facing equivalent (Dynamics 365 Sales).
