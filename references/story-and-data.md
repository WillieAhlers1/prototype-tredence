# Story and data

## Start with the business shift

Tredence's last-mile point of view still applies: show the everyday decision that changes, not only the
insight or dashboard. The redesigned business is credible because someone can act differently.

A prototype that only shows one person getting through a task faster is a productivity demo; one that shows the
same business covering more is an operations upgrade.  Aim higher: show how the business itself could change once
people and agents work together, and what that would be worth.  The person's day is how the room sees it; the
business shift is why they care.

**Go wide first.**  Write at least three ideas in `brief.md` before you choose: the one this customer would
expect, one that stretches them, and one that changes what the business is.  Use what you know about their
industry (its economics, its customers, its regulators, its competitors) and the questions below.  Your first
idea is the one everyone has.  Pick the boldest one their leaders could believe and real work on screen can
prove, and keep the others for the card's "Also considered" line.

**Then answer these in `brief.md` before the storyboard:**

- **The tension.**  What limits this business today?  Not a task pain, a business constraint: who gets served and
  who doesn't, how fast it can respond, what it costs to serve, what slips through, what it can't offer.  Name it
  in their words.
- **Why it's stuck.**  Usually the work behind it is manual, scarce or slow, so the business rations it: the top
  accounts get attention, a sample gets checked, the customer waits.
- **The turn.**  What changes when agents do that work and people keep the decisions.  The rationing stops, and
  then what does the business do with that?
- **The new shape.**  How the business could work, in operating terms (what it offers, who it serves, how it
  earns, what its people do), next to how it works today.  Not features.
- **The work, before and after.**  The process today and redesigned, as two short lists or a small table: each
  step and who does it; the steps that disappear or merge; the hand-offs that collapse; who owns what now; where
  a person decides.  This is how the shift actually happens, and it's the part the room will test first.  It's
  thinking for `brief.md`, not a screen: let the redesign show wherever it shows best.
- **The measure that moves**, roughly how far, and its basis (volume, minutes, rate, your assumption).  Revenue,
  growth, retention, cost to serve, cycle time, risk caught, customer experience.
- **Who owns it.**  The leader accountable for that measure.  They are the real audience, even when the story
  follows someone else.

**Show it, don't just say it.**  The new business and the redesigned work have to happen on screen somewhere, not
only appear in the close.  How is yours: the customer's own customer receiving what they couldn't get before, the
new offer in use, a role working differently, the old hand-off simply not happening, a view you design.  Test:
point to the moment the room *sees* the new shape.  If you can only point to the close or the presenter's words,
it's a claim, not a demo.

### Questions that break the frame

Ask them about this customer and answer in their terms.  Keep going past the first answer.

- If this work cost almost nothing, what would they start doing that they ration today, and what would they stop
  doing altogether?
- What do their customers want and can't get today: the attention, speed, advice or certainty only the
  best-served receive?  What would it take to give it to all of them, all the time?
- What could they sell that they can't deliver by hand: a service, an insight, a guarantee, something built on
  what they already know?
- What would a competitor founded this year, with agents from day one, do to them?
- What could their best people do if the groundwork vanished, and how would their jobs change shape?
- Where does their knowledge sit unused (records, conversations, history), and who would value it?
- If their whole industry worked this way in five years, what would the leaders look like, and how does this
  customer get there first?

**Test the idea.**  If it's the same business doing the same things earlier, faster or for more people, it's an
upgrade, not a new business.  If a competitor wouldn't worry and their CEO wouldn't repeat it, push again.  If
real work on screen can't show it, bring it down to earth until it can: bold, not vague.  And keep it theirs: an
idea any company could claim isn't one yet.

### Two heights

The room needs to understand the shift **on the ground** (real work changing: a person, a team, their customer, a
case, a decision someone makes) and **across the business** (what that means for the whole book, the region, the
network, the measure).  That's an understanding, not two scenes or a set of screens.  One moment can carry both:
the customer's own customer receiving something new, a role that works differently, a map, a line in the
presenter's voice.  A leader's report is one way among many.  Without the wider view, the room sees a faster day.
Without the ground, they see a claim.

### When the notes are a list of capabilities

Customers and colleagues often hand over a list of features or areas to cover.  Don't build one scene per item,
and don't tick them off at the end.  Ask what business result the list is for, tell that story, and let each
capability show up where it matters.  Keep a mapping of items to scenes in `brief.md` for the presenter, not on
screen.  If an item doesn't fit the story, say so in your reply rather than bolting on a scene.

## The storyboard

A prototype is a short story told through product screens.  The room should see the business as it is, the
work changing with people still in charge, and what that lets the business become.  The order, the pace and the
point of view are yours.

**Then find the story on the ground.**  Answer these in `brief.md`:

- **Whose work shows the shift**: one person, a team, a hand-off between people, the customer's own customer,
  or several at once?  Where does that work start today (an inbox, a phone in the field, a meeting, a queue in
  their own system, a website)?
- **What's slow, risky or tedious about it now?**  Name it in their words.
- **What does the agent take off their plate, and what do they still decide?**
- **What is the one moment that makes this customer lean in?**
- **What should the room say, do or ask for when the last scene goes down?**

**Then one sentence**: the shift, and the moment that shows it: who, what the agent does, what the person
decides, and what changes for the business.  Say it aloud.  If you can't say it in one sentence, the prototype
will wander.

**Then the beats**: what the room must see happen, in order.  **Then the scene table** (in `brief.md`), choosing
for each beat the screen that shows it best (`look-and-feel.md` §What screens mean).  The format:

| # | Screen | Presenter says (one line, out loud) | On screen | Click |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | | | | |
| … | | | | |

There is no default sequence of screens, no required screen, and no set length.  Open where the tension is
clearest or where the work really starts, give the signature moment the view that shows it best, and end where
the idea lands.  Depth beats novelty: never thin a story to make it look different from an earlier one.

**Craft notes.**  What usually makes a demo land, and why.  Break any of them when the story is better for it,
and say why in `brief.md`.

- **As many scenes as the story needs to be shown, not told.**  How many is your call.  A demo is not a slide
  deck: if seeing the follow-through, the proof in the target system, or the hand-off to a second person makes
  the story believable, give it its own scene.  Show, don't explain.
- **Pace for the room**: one idea per scene, long enough for the room to take it in.  In a long demo, a chapter
  break (`065.jpg`) between acts helps the room keep its place.
- **People stay in charge, visibly.**  The room should see a person decide where it matters: confirm, edit,
  approve, choose, or set the rule the agent follows.  Not every scene needs a decision; anything approval-class
  always waits for one (`look-and-feel.md` §Who acts).
- **Open where the work starts.**  Copilot Home, a phone in the field, an email arriving, a Teams meeting, the
  customer's own app or website: whatever is true for this work.  Employees work with the agent in the Copilot
  app, with business apps coming to them (sources, the Workspace pane, the browser pane); other apps appear where
  the work starts, as proof, or to show people reached.  Two or three supporting views usually beat a tour.
- **Make it believable.**  The room trusts what it sees happen: what the agent read and checked, its sources, and,
  when it matters, where the result landed (a record, a reply, a calendar, the customer's own screen).  Show the
  work before the result, or open on the result and let the person look underneath; either works.
- **A signature moment**: the thing only this story has, that makes this customer lean in.  Give it a view
  designed for it, build the beat timing around it, and give it the `.ai-touched` treatment.
- **Sound it out.**  Read every presenter line and every on-screen sentence aloud.  Calm, specific, honest.  If a
  line would make the architect wince ("our AI eliminates manual work"), rewrite it.

### Finding how the story moves

There is no list of story types to pick from.  Ask these about this customer's work, and let the answers shape
the scenes:

- How does the work arrive, and who notices first: a person, a customer, a signal nobody is watching?
- What makes it hard in real life (data that's missing or late, sources or people that disagree, a rule that
  blocks the easy answer), and who has to work through it?
- What does the person actually weigh, change or overrule?
- What happens while nobody is looking, overnight or between meetings, and what's waiting when they return?
- Who else is in the moment: a colleague, a manager, the customer on the other side?  Would the room see more
  from their side?
- Who receives the result, and what changes for them?
- Where does the work pass between people or teams today, and what gets lost there?
- What does the room need to see side by side to feel the difference: before and after, two people, two places?
- If the agent runs a whole process, what is the customer's real process, step by step, and where must a person
  decide?  Write it in `brief.md` first (each step, who acts and why, what proof it leaves), from how they really
  work, not from what looks impressive; a few well-placed stops make the point, a stop at every step is a form.
- What happens next: the second case, the next week, the rest of the book?
- What's the one beat that, cut, would make the story fall apart?  Build around it.

### The customer's own way of working (optional)

In Cowork, the customer can teach Copilot how *they* do a task: a **skill**, plain-language instructions their own
experts write (steps, rules, thresholds, formats, the checks they never skip, what always goes to a person).  The
Copilot app shows skills, and the plugins that package them, under "Plugins".  Cowork picks a skill up on its own
when the task fits its description.

**Why it can matter to the story.**  It answers the two questions a doubting room asks: "how would it know how *we*
do it?" (the HOW: it follows the method their people wrote) and "who's in charge?" (the WHERE: people write it, own
it, change it and decide who gets it, and Copilot still stops for their approval).  Sometimes it *is* the business
shift: the way the best person works becomes the way the whole team works.

**Use it when** the work runs on a house method, a review standard, policy thresholds, a required format, or
judgement only a few experts hold today, or when consistency across a team is the point.  **Leave it out** when the
story is a single question about data, when the method is generic, or when the scene would only add time.  Never add
it because the feature exists.  One or two moments are enough.

**Ways to show it** (pick at most one or two; draw them in your own way):

- *In passing*: a step in Copilot's trace ("Reviewed custom skill …", with the skill's own icon), one of its rules
  applied by name in the thinking, and the skill in the Workspace under Plugins.  Costs no extra scene.
- *The owner shapes it*: the expert creates it in a Cowork conversation (by asking Cowork, or Customize › Skills ›
  Add › Create new: a guided conversation that drafts the skill as they talk and saves it to their OneDrive), Cowork
  says how well it's written, and they share it with their team (Share › Specific users; Re-share after a change).
  Or *teach it once*: Copilot gets something wrong the house way, the person edits the skill's instructions on its
  page, and from the next session it's right for everyone it's shared with.
- *Where it lives*: Customize › Skills, their skill under "Your skills" next to the built-in ones
  (`assets/shells.html`, `customize`).

**Write it in the data sheet**: a short kebab-case name (the way a skill is named), a description that says what it
does and when to use it, the rules the story relies on, its owner (from the cast), who it's shared with,
and its icon colour.  Use the rules' exact words everywhere they appear.

**Keep it honest**: the skill in the demo is fictitious, like the data.  A skill is instructions, not a guarantee;
approvals still stop for a person.  A skill's own page and Cowork's evaluation of a skill (its Skill Report) aren't
pictured on Learn: if the story needs them, keep them plain (the report as Copilot's reply in the conversation: a
verdict and one or two improvements) and say in the notes that the layout is illustrative.  Custom skills don't
work in Cowork on mobile, and an
organisation may need to approve plugins before people can use them; say so if asked.

## Starting from an existing HTML file or app

- Large single-file apps keep their content as data inside the script.  Pull that data out with a short Python
  script instead of reading the whole file.
- Use the card in `SKILL.md` (step 1), with the file's answers filled in, plus up to two lines it raises: which
  story to tell from the file and how it should end.
- If the file already uses its own fictional names, keep them unless the card says otherwise (add a line "Names:
  keep the file's").
- Leave out the file's own banners and disclaimers; the concept label covers them.
- Rebuild the scenes with the kit's classes and the product outlines (`assets/shells.html`) rather than copying
  the old markup, so the result looks and packs like every other prototype.

## The data sheet

Write the cast and the numbers once, in `brief.md`, and reuse them exactly.  Inconsistent data (a deal that's
$1.2M in Outlook and $1.4M in Dynamics) is the fastest way to lose the room.

```
Shift:    from [how the business works today] → to [how it could work]
Measure:  [measure] · today [value] → with agents [value] · illustrative · basis: [volume, minutes, rate, assumption]
Owner:    [role] (the leader accountable for the measure)
Company:  [fictitious customer] · [industry] · [size]
People:   [name], [role] (whose work changes)
          [name], [role]   (one line per person on screen)
Records:  [record type] "[name]" · [the fields the screens show, with their exact values]
Dates:    "today" = [weekday date]; [the other dates the story mentions]
Counts:   [what the agent read, found, drafted and changed, as numbers]
Charts:   [report] · [visual] · [series] · [categories] · [values] · [format]
```

### Making data feel real

- **Specific over round.**  $1,240,000, not $1M.  6 emails, not "all your emails".  "Since 28 Oct".  (Records and
  counts; impact estimates are rounded, below.)
- **Realistic texture.**  A mix of read and unread mail, a few older items, a field or two that's blank, a task
  with no owner yet.  Perfect data looks fake.
- **Dates line up** with "today" in the data sheet; times look like working hours; weekdays match dates.
- **Industry-true artefacts.**  Claims, work orders, policies, SKUs, shifts: use the customer's vocabulary.
- **Business impact is shown, honestly.**  The measure that moves appears where it fits the story (in a view
  you design, on a frame scene, in the presenter's words), rounded ("about 3× the clients covered", "days, not
  weeks"), labelled "Illustrative" wherever it's on screen, with its basis in `brief.md` and the presenter notes.  Plausible
  enough that a sceptical finance lead nods.  Never present it as measured: an estimate inside a Power BI report
  reads as data.  Time freed is capacity for more or better work, not a cash saving or a cut in staff.
  Task-level numbers (minutes saved on one step) support the shift; they aren't the headline.
- **Their data looks like Power BI.**  When a scene shows a trend, gap or breakdown from data the customer would
  really have, it's a Power BI visual from a named report.  The number in the sentence, the visual, its tooltip
  and the report page are the same number from the data sheet.  One message per visual; put the series,
  categories and format in the data sheet.  Projections and "with agents" scenarios aren't report data (above).
  A figure inside a document the agent drafts is drawn in the Office theme's colours: one accent for the point,
  grey for the rest, no red and green (`look-and-feel.md` §Data colours).  No scene needs a chart.

**The customer in the room.**  Their name can go on the cover's client tag only when the user confirms it's allowed
(otherwise "Concept" or the industry).  Inside the scenes, the business is always one of the fictitious companies,
even when the story is about the customer's own situation.

## Words

The Tredence customer voice applies to every word on screen and in the notes (`tredence-frame.md` §Voice): start
in the customer's world, say what changes in someone's day, "we" and "you" together, confident but never
promotional, no exclamation marks.

- Short, plain, specific.  Product UI text is sentence case and functional ("Confirm plan", not "Let's go!").
- The agent speaks like a careful colleague: what it did, what it found, what it needs from you.  It admits gaps.
- Banned: unlock, unleash, seamless, leverage, utilise, supercharge, revolutionise, game-changing, empower,
  next-gen, cutting-edge, harness, delve, synergy, journey, flywheel, ideate, operationalise, best-in-class,
  world-class, magic.  The check flags them.
- Write "Tredence", never "TREDENCE" (that's the logo), "Tredence Inc." or "Tredence Analytics".  Tredence's own
  products and accelerators appear only if the user asks, described by what they do, never as a working product.
- No Tredence client names, client results, quotes or awards, even as background colour.  Proof points belong in a
  `pptx-tredence` deck, sourced and dated, not in a concept demo.
- Real product names exactly: Microsoft Copilot, Microsoft Teams, Outlook, Dynamics 365 Sales, Copilot Studio,
  Microsoft Fabric, Power BI, SharePoint.  Never invent product names or features as if they ship.
- Names change; check the current one before you build.  The Microsoft 365 Copilot app became **Microsoft
  Copilot** in August 2026 (copilot.cloud.microsoft): never write "Microsoft 365 Copilot" or "M365 Copilot".
  The Microsoft 365 subscription and Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and Teams keep their names.
- Internal Microsoft systems (MSX and similar) only for an internal audience, and only if the user asks.  For a
  customer, show the customer-facing equivalent (Dynamics 365 Sales).
